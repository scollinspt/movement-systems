# 2. Central Intellectual Position

Movement Systems should explicitly embrace a **dynamical systems, complex adaptive systems, ecological dynamics, and constraints-based approach to understanding human movement**.

This perspective has been part of Sean Collins's intellectual trajectory for decades, including research involving:

- Complex systems and clinical research.
- Environmental stress and physiological regulation.
- Ambulatory physiological measurement.
- Variability in autonomic control.
- Stability, decompensation, and adaptation.
- Systems theory and causal reasoning in DPT education.
- Movement as an emergent functional phenomenon.

The new website should make this continuity visible.

Avoid presenting ecological dynamics as a recent change in philosophy or as a fashionable replacement for traditional motor-control terminology.

Instead, present it as an increasingly explicit movement-science application of a longstanding commitment to studying **complex, adaptive, multiscale, open biological systems in context**.

## Scientific premises

Develop site content around the following premises.

### A. Movement is emergent

Human movement emerges from interactions among physiological, neural, muscular, mechanical, perceptual, behavioral, task, environmental, and historical constraints.

A movement pattern cannot generally be explained by a single impairment or isolated component.

The movement visible to an observer is a realized solution produced by a complex interacting system.

### B. Movement is goal-directed and context-dependent

The scientific unit of interest is not merely a body segment moving through space.

It is an organism attempting to accomplish a functional task within a particular environment.

The same functional task may be accomplished through different movement strategies.

The same observed movement strategy may arise from different underlying mechanisms.

### C. Movement is dynamically organized

Coordination can emerge through the interactions of coupled components.

Relevant constructs include:

- Coordination patterns.
- Relative phase.
- Coupling.
- Order parameters.
- Control parameters.
- Attractors.
- Stability and loss of stability.
- Transitions between coordination patterns.
- Multistability.
- Variability.
- Self-organization.
- Adaptation.

Use these terms precisely. They are not interchangeable metaphors.

For example, changing gait variability is not automatically evidence of a phase transition, and a low-variability movement pattern is not automatically stable or healthy.

### D. Movement is constrained by available dynamic resources

Drawing particularly on Holt, Wagenaar, and Saltzman (2010), distinguish the organization of movement from the mechanical resources contributing to its production.

Dynamic resources include:

- Active, appropriately timed muscular force production.
- Elastic energy storage and return.
- Pendular exchanges of kinetic and gravitational potential energy.
- Segment mass, length, and inertia.
- Damping and mechanical energy dissipation.
- Physiological energy supply.

These resources interact with anatomical structure, task demands, environmental forces, and coordination dynamics.

A person's observed movement pattern may represent an adaptation to a changed set of resources rather than an incorrectly executed ideal movement.

### E. Perception and action are coupled

Drawing on ecological psychology, especially James J. Gibson and subsequent ecological dynamics scholarship, recognize that movement is guided by information about possibilities for action.

Important constructs include:

- Affordances.
- Effectivities or action capabilities.
- Perception–action coupling.
- Information available in the environment.
- Exploratory behavior.
- Body-scaled and action-scaled relationships.
- Task-relevant opportunities for action.

Do not collapse ecological psychology into biomechanics.

Although biomechanical measurement can characterize movement, ecological hypotheses about affordance perception and action selection may require additional observations and experimental manipulations.

### F. Stability and adaptability must be investigated across timescales

Movement and physiological regulation involve different timescales:

- Milliseconds and individual contractions.
- Individual movement cycles.
- Continuous task performance.
- Repeated performances.
- Hours and days.
- Learning and training.
- Recovery and rehabilitation.
- Development and aging.
- Long-term participation.

A system may exhibit apparent stability at one timescale while changing at another.

The website should encourage investigations of temporal organization rather than focusing exclusively on averaged movement measurements.

### G. Observation is not explanation

This must remain a defining principle of the website.

Sensors produce measurements.

Measurements support descriptions and estimates.

Models connect measurements to possible mechanisms.

Experimental manipulation and convergent evidence help discriminate among competing explanations.

A plausible mechanistic interpretation is not equivalent to proof of that mechanism.

Every research project should make this distinction explicit.

## Movement Systems Mission

**Movement Systems advances the scientific understanding of human movement as an emergent, adaptive phenomenon arising from interactions among biological systems, individual capabilities, tasks, and environments. Through the Human Movement Systems Laboratory, we develop, evaluate, and share accessible measurement technologies, computational models, experimental methods, and clinical reasoning approaches that connect movement observations to testable explanations and meaningful functional outcomes.**

# 6. Attain–Sustain–Maintain as a Cross-Cutting Functional Framework

Integrate Sean Collins's Attain–Sustain–Maintain approach.

This is a developing conceptual framework associated with his scholarly direction. It should not be attributed to Gibson, Kelso, Holt, or Guccione unless a specific source warrants the claim.

## Attain

The capacity of an interacting system to achieve a specified functional state or task outcome under identifiable constraints.

Example:

Muscle fibers contribute force with appropriate timing.

At a higher scale, the resulting forces and mechanical interactions permit a limb to achieve the movement necessary for standing.

At another scale, the person successfully stands from a chair.

## Sustain

The capacity to preserve necessary task performance or functional organization for the required duration, despite ongoing internal and external demands.

Example:

The person remains standing or continues walking for a task-relevant interval while managing fatigue, perturbations, and changing environmental conditions.

## Maintain

The capacity to retain, recover, or adapt the resources and coordination necessary for repeated functional achievement over longer periods.

Example:

The person maintains the ability to walk independently across repeated days, changing physical conditions, rehabilitation, disease progression, and aging.

## Important distinctions

These categories are not necessarily sequential.

They can operate simultaneously at different scales.

Maintaining function does not necessarily mean maintaining an identical movement pattern.

Adaptation may preserve functional capability by changing coordination patterns.

An individual may attain a task using a solution that is difficult to sustain or that imposes excessive long-term costs.

Build a dedicated conceptual page and an accessible diagram showing:

- Interacting organism, task, and environmental constraints.
- Nested biological and functional levels.
- Attain, Sustain, and Maintain as recurring functional requirements.
- Temporal scales.
- Dynamic resources and adaptability.

Present this as a provisional conceptual synthesis requiring further theoretical clarification and empirical development.

# 8. Reorganize Research Around Scientific Questions

The main research portfolio should begin with a scientific phenomenon and research question, not a device.

Create a hierarchy:

**Research Theme → Research Question → Theoretical Prediction → Candidate Mechanisms → Required Measurements → Method Development → Experiment → Analysis → Interpretation → Result**

## Proposed Research Theme A: Emergence and Organization of Coordination

Central question:

How do interacting organismic, task, and environmental constraints give rise to preferred movement patterns?

Potential questions:

- How does gait coordination change with speed?
- How do interlimb relationships change when mechanical demands are altered?
- Which coordination patterns remain stable under perturbation?
- Are observed transitions gradual or discontinuous?
- Can simplified coupled-oscillator models predict experimentally observed changes?

## Theme B: Movement Variability, Stability, and Adaptability

Central question:

Under what conditions does movement variability reflect functional adaptability, exploration, instability, or loss of control?

Avoid using variability as a general-purpose biomarker without a construct-specific justification.

Distinguish:

- Amount of variability.
- Temporal structure of variability.
- Stability of coordination.
- Adaptability to changing conditions.
- Response to perturbation.
- Ability to transition between coordination patterns.

## Theme C: Dynamic Resources and Mechanical Constraints

Central question:

How do available mechanical and physiological resources influence emergent movement solutions?

Consider:

- Force-production timing.
- Stiffness.
- Inertia.
- Damping.
- Energy storage and return.
- Mechanical work.
- Physiological energy supply.
- Functional cost and tradeoffs.

Link measurements to mathematical or biomechanical models that produce testable predictions.

## Theme D: Ecological Perception–Action Research

Central question:

How do perceived opportunities for action interact with movement capabilities and task demands?

Candidate investigations:

- Body-scaled obstacle negotiation.
- Locomotion through constrained spaces.
- Step-height affordances.
- Surface selection.
- Walking speed selection.
- Movement exploration under changing task conditions.

IMUs may measure resulting movement, but investigations of perception and affordance judgments require appropriate additional methods.

## Theme E: Functional Capability Across Time

Central question:

How do individuals attain, sustain, and maintain functional performance as constraints and available resources change?

Investigate:

- Task initiation.
- Continuous performance.
- Fatigue-related adaptation.
- Recovery.
- Repeated-task capability.
- Motor learning.
- Longitudinal functional change.

## Theme F: Physiological Regulation and Movement

Connect earlier autonomic research with current questions.

Explore how physiological resources and regulatory responses interact with movement performance.

Possible future multimodal measurements may include:

- IMUs.
- ECG.
- Heart rate and heart-rate variability.
- Respiration.
- Force and pressure.
- Video and kinematics.
- Task and environmental descriptors.

These should be developed only where required by a research hypothesis.

# 13. Proposed Flagship Experiment

Develop a methodological concept page for an initial coordination-dynamics experiment.

Working title:

**Measurement of Constraint-Dependent Coordination in Human Locomotion Using Wearable Inertial Sensors**

Scientific question:

Can direct IMU measurements characterize reproducible changes in locomotor coordination as selected task constraints change?

Initial experimental approach:

Use controlled rhythmic tasks and walking to develop and evaluate synchronized measurements of intersegment timing and relative phase.

Possible variables:

- Movement frequency.
- Walking speed.
- Segment angular velocity.
- Relative phase.
- Stride-cycle characteristics.
- Coordination variability.
- Responses to controlled changes in task demands.

Initial scientific objectives:

1. Establish reliable timing and synchronization.
2. Compare alternative phase estimation methods.
3. Evaluate reproducibility.
4. Characterize sensitivity to processing choices.
5. Determine whether observed coordination changes are reproducible.
6. Establish what additional measurements are required to evaluate stability or identify mechanisms.

The experiment should not initially claim to demonstrate attractors or dynamical phase transitions.

Those are stronger scientific propositions requiring specifically designed tests.

All human involvement must depend on appropriate institutional determination, research governance, and consent where required.

# 22. Critical Scientific Safeguards

Implement editorial safeguards throughout.

### Avoid theoretical overclaiming

Ecological dynamics is an important scientific perspective, but not an empirically settled explanation of all motor control.

Present alternative explanations where appropriate.

### Avoid mechanism claims based on measurement alone

Kinematics do not directly identify force production, stiffness, metabolic expenditure, neural control, or affordance perception.

### Avoid inappropriate clinical claims

A proposed movement metric is not automatically a diagnostic biomarker.

A prototype is not automatically a clinical device.

A feasibility study is not validation.

### Avoid conflating variability and adaptability

A change in variability may have multiple causes and functional meanings.

### Avoid conflating individual and population inference

Population findings can guide patient-specific hypotheses but do not uniquely determine an individual's mechanisms.

### Avoid conflating scientific and technical maturity

Working code does not imply valid measurement.

Valid measurement does not imply clinical usefulness.

Clinical usefulness does not establish superiority to existing practice.

### Avoid misrepresenting collaborators

Only display verified affiliations, permissions, and collaborations.

# 25. Definition of Success

**What is this research program trying to understand?**

How human movement emerges, becomes coordinated, adapts, and supports meaningful function through interactions among organismic, task, and environmental constraints.

**What theories guide the research?**

Dynamical systems, complex adaptive systems, coordination dynamics, ecological psychology, ecological dynamics, biomechanical modeling, and causal scientific reasoning.

**What does the HMS Lab actually develop?**

Measurement systems, validated methods, computational models, experimental approaches, reproducible software, and evidence that can improve explanations of movement.

**Why use wearable sensors?**

To obtain scientifically relevant observations of movement under specified conditions, not simply because new sensor technology is available.

**How does the laboratory distinguish measurement from mechanism?**

Through explicit constructs, model assumptions, competing explanations, validation, systematic manipulation of constraints, and careful interpretation of uncertainty.

**How is this connected to physical therapy?**

By strengthening the scientific understanding of functional movement and helping clinicians reason about movement, adaptation, and intervention in the context of individual patients.

**What makes this laboratory distinctive?**

The integration of a longstanding multiscale systems-science trajectory, clinically grounded questions, ecological and dynamical theories of movement, accessible instrumentation, open development, and a rigorous pathway from observation to defensible explanation.
