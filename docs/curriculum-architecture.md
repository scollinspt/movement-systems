# Curriculum Architecture

## Program Frame

The Plymouth State DPT curriculum uses causation, systems, and adaptation as broad
concepts through which theoretical knowledge is developed and applied to practice. The
supplied curriculum concepts document describes theory as supporting information for the
whole complex task of patient/client management and describes the curriculum as a
scaffold that accumulates complexity over time.

This project applies that program frame rather than treating Movement Systems and
Exercise Prescription & Nutrition as isolated content areas.

## Course Structure

Movement Systems is a 4-credit course that includes the shared Movement and Exercise
Laboratory. EP&N is a 3-credit course and supplies the primary content basis for that
laboratory. The same DPT cohort takes both courses for 7 total credits, allowing their
concepts, cases, and applied work to be coordinated directly.

The laboratory is not a separately registered course. There are two course syllabi, not
three: the Movement Systems syllabus includes the laboratory, and the EP&N syllabus
references the laboratory. The same laboratory schedule appears in both syllabi.

## Complementary Course Roles

### Movement Systems: Systems Theory

Movement Systems studies human movement through the focusing lens of systems theory. It
addresses:

- biomechanics, neuromechanics, neuromuscular control, motor control, and motor learning;
- hierarchical organization and interactions across scales;
- constraints, initial conditions, and context;
- regulation, control, and feedback;
- variability, stability, stationarity, stasis, and equilibrium;
- nonlinearity and emergence;
- adaptation of movement behavior and system organization; and
- explicit models as aids to reasoning rather than as the system itself.

### Exercise Prescription & Nutrition: Knowledge Foundations

EP&N develops knowledge needed to understand and influence exercise response and
adaptation. It addresses:

- physiological support for movement and exercise;
- exercise dose, workload, acute response, and chronic adaptation;
- strength, endurance, mobility, recovery, and progression;
- energetics and nutritional support;
- individualization, monitoring, safety, and modification; and
- causal mechanisms and evidence that support prescription decisions.

Instructor-specific lecture artifacts remain separate from this conceptual role. The
course can participate fully in the shared architecture without placing every lecture
deck on the public site.

## Shared Conceptual Spine

### Systems

Students reason about interacting parts, nested scales, environmental relationships,
and emergent capabilities. Physiological mechanisms and movement behavior are related
without collapsing one level into another.

### Adaptation

Movement Systems considers adaptation as dynamic system behavior across interacting
levels. EP&N supplies mechanisms and dose-response knowledge used to induce, monitor,
and interpret adaptation. The laboratory makes these relationships observable and
actionable.

### Causation

Causal reasoning connects evidence, models, patient particulars, and intervention
choices. Students should distinguish observation from explanation, association from
causation, and model prediction from clinical validation.

## Relationship To The Clinical Inquiry Ecosystem

The curricular pathway and the scholarly ecosystem are related but not identical. In the
curriculum, students learn to connect evidence, physiological mechanisms, movement-system
knowledge, and patient particulars. In the scholarly ecosystem, reviewed movement-domain
claims and models from this work can join evidence and physiological mechanisms as inputs
to Models4PT population causal knowledge; the Clinical Inference Engine then owns the
separate task of using population knowledge in patient-specific reasoning.

This relationship is bidirectional. Course cases, laboratories, and model use can generate
research questions, while reviewed ecosystem outputs can return as inspectable teaching
representations. Neither direction permits a pedagogical simplification, simulation
result, or population association to become a clinical conclusion without the required
review and contextual reasoning.

The detailed scholarly role and contribution requirements are defined in
[clinical-inquiry-integration.md](clinical-inquiry-integration.md).

## Curricular Dependencies

Students enter with relevant physiology, neuroscience, and Dynamic Systems I
foundations. Movement Systems and EP&N coordinate with PCM III and should establish
knowledge and reasoning that can be reused with greater complexity in PCM IV and V.

The project must eventually document these relationships in a matrix with at least:

- concept or capability;
- prerequisite expectation;
- Movement Systems contribution;
- EP&N contribution;
- shared interactive or laboratory application;
- PCM III application; and
- expected transfer to PCM IV-V.

## Design Rules

- Organize learning around concepts and consequential questions, not platform features.
- Identify whether an activity develops knowledge, systems reasoning, procedure, or
  whole-task practice.
- Use cases repeatedly so complexity accumulates rather than resetting by course.
- Keep simplifications traceable to deeper explanations and evidence.
- State the scale, timescale, assumptions, and limits of every model.
- Evaluate integration through students' reasoning and decisions, not merely exposure to
  content from both lectures.
